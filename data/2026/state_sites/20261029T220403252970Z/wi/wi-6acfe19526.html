Wisconsin's Election FAQs

In today’s fast-paced information environment, Wisconsinites have been inundated with claims about the state’s elections system on social media and elsewhere. Many of these claims are not accurate. Other claims may provide information that is mostly accurate but lack context. The Wisconsin Elections Commission takes seriously all election-related claims that are concerning to Wisconsin voters.

    How is absentee voting data reported, and what do I need to know when analyzing the statistics?

    The WEC posts daily absentee ballot report spreadsheets leading up to an election. There are two spreadsheets generated by the system at 7:30 a.m. each day, one broken down by county and another by municipality. You can access those reports here: https://elections.wi.gov/statistics-data/absentee-statistics. 

    There are individual pages, based on the election, where the daily absentee reports are posted. To access a given election, go to that year and you’ll find what you need.

    Q: What do the columns in the spreadsheet mean?
    The first three columns of numbers represent all absentee ballots. The last column (Column G) is for in-person absentee ballots and is a direct subset of the third column. (See more below for additional clarity). 

    Q: How should media report absentee voting numbers?
    Members of the media should use column “F: Ballots Returned” when reporting how many people voted early, ahead of Election Day. This data includes all absentee ballots returned to the clerk either by mail, in person, or placed in a drop box. In addition, this data includes voters who cast a ballot during In-Person Absentee Voting (IPAV) hours.

    Use column “G: In-person absentee” when reporting only on voters who went to a voting location ahead of Election Day, known as In-Person Absentee Voting (IPAV). NEVER add F & G columns together. Column G is a subset/filtered version of column F. Additional questions? Reach out to the Public Information Office at @email.

    Q: Does the data show the political party affiliation of voters requesting absentee ballots?
    No. This data doesn’t exist because Wisconsin does not register voters by political party. 

    Q: Where does the data come from?
    The data is based on entries made by municipal clerks within Wisconsin’s statewide voter registration system. Note: More than 1,000 of the 1,850+ municipal clerks in Wisconsin do not have direct access to the system due to their size and rely on their county clerk or a neighboring clerk to enter data on their behalf.

    The data also tracks requests and ballots, not voters. For example, some voters submit multiple requests for an absentee ballot or request a replacement ballot. In these instances, clerks only count one ballot per voter. 

    What is the new USPS by mail voting rule, and how does it impact Wisconsin?

    In March 2026, President Trump issued an Executive Order directing USPS to develop an administrative rule that would regulate federal absentee voting by mail. USPS finalized and published that rule on Wednesday, August 26, 2026. The rule applies nationwide to any state that uses the USPS to send or receive absentee ballots. 

    The rule proposed a system where all states would use a specific ballot envelope containing personalized barcodes for absentee voters. States would have to individually upload voter information for each by mail absentee voter (except military and overseas voters) into a USPS absentee voter portal prior to sending the absentee ballots out. Then, USPS would scan the barcodes on the envelopes to determine if the ballot can or cannot be delivered to the voter by USPS. Under the rule, which is now blocked by a federal court, USPS would not deliver any absentee ballots it could not verify through its portal. 

    You can read the full text of the rule here: https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-17238.pdf 

    USPS has also developed an FAQ for its rule that it issued before the rule was blocked. You can read that here, but please note it contains outdated information and the provisions of the rule described are not currently in effect: https://about.usps.com/what/government-services/election-mail/pdf/faq-usps-final-rule-ballot-mail-federal-elections.pdf 

    Is the USPS rule currently in effect? 
    No. On Thursday, August 27, a federal court entered an order blocking the rule, and USPS is currently unable to enforce it or compel states to comply with it.

    What did the court do? 
    The federal court blocked all mandatory components of the USPS rule. The court said that while USPS could continue to build the portal and develop these systems, no state can be compelled to use them. 

    Can USPS refuse to deliver absentee ballots if states don’t follow the rule? 
    No. Because enforcement of the rule is currently blocked by a federal court, USPS must continue to mail absentee ballots as usual. 

    Will Wisconsin comply with the rule even if it's not in effect? 
    No. Wisconsin cannot comply for several reasons. First, the USPS portal does not exist. USPS has given no indication of when or whether it will exist, and USPS has provided no training on how the portal will be used. The USPS rule is not a simple administrative change. It proposes a brand-new, highly complex, untested system involving new carrier envelope design, information coded into Intelligent Mail barcodes, a USPS-run database of voter information (input by clerks), none of which the Commission or clerks have been trained to develop or use. The USPS rule was developed and published in under five months, and USPS has not issued any training or explanation of how this system will actually work. 

    Second, the first batches of absentee ballots for the November election will be going out across the country starting the week of August 31, including in Wisconsin. It is too late for Wisconsin, or any state, to make significant changes to election law, policy, or procedure because voting is underway. 

    Finally, state and federal caselaw prohibit Wisconsin from making major changes in close proximity to an ongoing election. Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549 U.S. 1 (2006); Hawkins v. Wisconsin Elections Commission, 2020 WI 75, 393 Wis. 2d 629, 948 N.W.2d 877. 

    What will happen next? Will anything change before the November election? 
    Likely not. The rule has been blocked while the litigation continues, and the judge that blocked the rule reasoned that the states challenging will likely be successful in arguing that the rule violates the law. While it’s possible that an appeals court could disagree with the federal judge who blocked the rule, the likelihood that any court will allow the rule to go back into effect lessens as we get closer to Election Day. This is because federal courts in particular, including the U.S. Supreme Court, are usually reluctant to change the rules for voting and procedure in close proximity to an election. Nothing is guaranteed, but the Commission will provide timely updates if something changes.
     

    Will USPS' new postmark rules impact Wisconsin voters?

    No. Unlike other states, Wisconsin is a “in-hand” ballot state.

    Absentee ballots must be in the hands of election officials by 8 p.m. on Election Day to be counted. This means USPS postmarks on absentee ballots are irrelevant. The time when the ballot was “in hand” by the clerk’s office is what determines a ballot’s status, not the postmark on the envelope in which it was mailed.

    If you’re planning to mail in your absentee ballot, the WEC recommends doing so at least 10 days before Election Day.

    Voters registering to vote by mail do need to have their mail postmarked 20 days before an election, see Wis. Stat. § 6.28(1)(a). You can also register to vote online or in person at your local clerk's office. Visit MyVote.wi.gov to learn more.

    What is a postmark?

    A postmark is a marking applied by the USPS to record the date, location, and zip code of where it was first processed.

    Could the electronic transmission of unofficial election results be hacked?

    Some theories postulate that a third-party could intercept the very brief results transmission, and break the encryption, and somehow alter the data to change election results.  This narrative fails to recognize many facets of the elections process. In addition to overcoming incredible technical challenges and achieving spectacularly precise timing, the theory ignores the fact that transmitted results are encrypted with a specific security key needed to pass through the voting system firewall and also ignores the fact that the tabulator results are printed on paper. 

    In addition, those hard copy results are further backed by paper ballots or a paper record for each and every vote.  Indeed, the herculean technical feat of “hacking” the results transmission may be easily undone by a simple phone call to verify the transmission – something many Wisconsin clerks already do.
